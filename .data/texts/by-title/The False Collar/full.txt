The False Collar

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


In a tidy drawer, among ribbons, buttons, and bits of lace, there lived a stiff white collar.

It was not part of a person.

It was only a collar.

But it had a very large opinion of itself.

“I am so fine,” the collar thought.

“So bright.

So proper.

So important.”

It had been worn once—only once—by someone who wanted to look impressive.

The collar remembered that day the way a vain creature remembers applause.

It remembered being fastened carefully.

It remembered being admired in the mirror.

It remembered how people said, “How sharp you look.”

Afterward, it was washed.

It was starched.

It was laid away.

And it waited.

Waiting is difficult for a proud collar.

To pass the time, it began to talk.

It spoke to the stocking.

It spoke to the garter.

It spoke to the ribbon.

And it told stories about itself.

“I have been around important people,” it said.

“I have been in great rooms.”

“I have seen fine dinners.”

“I belong with the very best.”

The ribbon listened quietly.

The stocking did not argue.

The garter merely lay there, useful and patient.

The collar did not like their silence.

It wanted admiration.

So it began to invent brighter stories.

“Once,” it said, “I was almost engaged.”

“Engaged?” asked the ribbon.

“Yes,” said the collar. “To someone elegant. Very elegant.”

It did not specify who.

It did not need truth; it needed an audience.

Then one day, the collar was taken out.

It was brushed.

It was smoothed.

It was made crisp.

The collar felt triumphant.

“At last,” it thought, “I will be seen.”

But when it was washed again, something happened.

The collar cracked.

Not dramatically.

Not loudly.

Just a small, stubborn crack that spoiled its perfect stiffness.

The collar was horrified.

“How can I be admired now?” it thought.

It tried to hold itself straight.

It tried to pretend nothing had changed.

But perfection, once broken, is difficult to pretend.

After that, the collar was used less.

People reached for softer things.

For simpler things.

For things that did their job without demanding applause.

The collar lay in the drawer and listened.

It listened to the stocking being chosen.

It listened to the ribbon being tied.

It listened to the quiet pride of usefulness.

And slowly the collar began to understand.

Being stiff and showy was not the same as being valuable.

Being admired for a moment was not the same as being needed.

In the end, the collar was tossed aside—not with cruelty, but with indifference.

And that indifference taught it the final lesson:

That the best kind of pride is quiet.

And the best kind of beauty is the kind that helps.

The End.

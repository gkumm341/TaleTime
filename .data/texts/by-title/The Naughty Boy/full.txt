The Naughty Boy

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


On a bright morning when the air smelled of summer, a poet sat by an open window.

He had paper before him.

He had a pen in his hand.

And he had the familiar problem of a poet: too many thoughts and not enough order.

He was trying to write something clever.

Something beautiful.

Something that would make people nod and say, “Ah, how wise.”

But his thoughts wandered.

Then there was a tap at the glass.

The poet looked up.

On the window ledge sat a small boy.

He did not look like an ordinary child.

He had a bow.

He had a quiver of arrows.

And his eyes sparkled with mischief.

The poet sighed.

“I know who you are,” he said. “You are Cupid—the naughty boy.”

Cupid grinned as if he had been praised.

“May I come in?” he asked.

The poet hesitated.

“If you promise not to cause trouble,” he said.

Cupid’s smile widened.

He hopped inside.

He looked around the room.

He examined the poet’s papers.

He poked at a book.

He sat in the poet’s chair as if it belonged to him.

The poet watched warily.

“People talk about you,” the poet said. “They say you shoot arrows into hearts and make sensible people do foolish things.”

Cupid tilted his head.

“They are correct,” he said.

“Then don’t shoot me,” the poet said quickly.

Cupid raised his bow.

“Don’t!” the poet cried.

But the arrow flew.

It did not strike the wall.

It did not strike the window.

It struck the poet’s heart.

The poet gasped.

Not because it hurt terribly—though it stung like a sudden truth.

But because everything inside him shifted.

Suddenly he remembered faces.

Smiles.

Kind voices.

He felt warmth.

He felt longing.

He felt as if his heart had been sleeping and had just been shaken awake.

Cupid laughed softly.

“That is what I do,” he said.

The poet tried to look stern.

He tried to be angry.

But it was difficult.

Because even as he complained, he felt more alive.

“Why are you so naughty?” he demanded.

Cupid shrugged.

“Because people forget,” he said.

“They think they can control everything.

They think they can plan their hearts like they plan their schedules.

But love arrives when it wants.

It is not polite.

It is not obedient.

It is powerful.”

With that, Cupid hopped back onto the window ledge.

“Wait!” the poet called. “Take it back!”

But Cupid only winked.

He vanished like a sunbeam.

The poet sat very still.

His clever lines were gone.

His tidy thoughts were scattered.

But his heart was full.

He began to write again.

Not just clever words.

Honest ones.

And he understood the lesson the naughty boy had brought:

That love can surprise us.

That love can soften us.

And that even trouble, sometimes, is the beginning of something good.

The End.

The Swineherd

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


Once there was a Prince who lived in a small kingdom where the gardens were well kept and the people were content. He was not the kind of Prince who wanted the loudest music or the greatest show. He wanted something quieter and rarer: a wife who was kind and true.

In a neighboring land lived a Princess who was said to be very grand. People spoke of her palace, her gowns, her mirrors, and her pride. But the Prince decided not to judge her only by rumor.

“Perhaps she has a gentle heart,” he told himself. “Perhaps she simply needs someone to see it.”

So he sent her gifts.

The first was a rose from his own garden—his finest rose, the one he had watched day by day as it opened.

The second was a nightingale whose song could quiet an angry room and soften a tired heart.

The messengers arrived at the Princess’s palace and presented the gifts.

The Princess looked at the rose.

“It is only a real rose,” she said, as if that were an insult.

Then the nightingale sang.

The Princess frowned.

“It is only a real bird,” she said.

And she sent the gifts away.

When the Prince heard this, he felt something that was not anger so much as disappointment.

“If she cannot value a rose and a song,” he thought, “what will she value?”

Still, he wished to see the Princess with his own eyes. But he did not go with trumpets and banners. He did not go with a glittering carriage. Instead, he put on humble clothes and traveled alone.

When he reached the palace gates, he bowed and asked for work.

The guards laughed.

“We have plenty of servants already,” they said.

But the palace always needed someone to do the lowest work. So at last they gave him a tiny room near the stables and a job caring for the pigs.

And so the Prince became, in that place, a swineherd.

Now the swineherd was not only clever; he was patient. In the evenings, when the yard grew quiet and the palace lights glowed high above, he made little things with his hands.

One night he made a small cooking pot. It was an ordinary pot in shape, but it was enchanted. If you held it to a fire until it began to simmer, it could tell you what was cooking in every kitchen throughout the city.

The swineherd set the pot on a stool outside his door.

The next morning, word reached the Princess.

“A pot that knows the kitchens?” she exclaimed. “Bring it to me.”

But she was told the swineherd would only sell it.

So the Princess, curious and proud, went down to the yard with her ladies-in-waiting.

“Swineherd,” she said, keeping her chin lifted, “how much for the pot?”

“Ten kisses,” the swineherd replied.

The Princess recoiled.

“Ten kisses! For a pot?”

“Ten,” said the swineherd calmly.

The Princess did not like the bargain. But she liked the pot. She liked the idea of having something no one else had, something clever that would make people look at her with surprise.

So she ordered her ladies to stand around her in a circle and shield her from view.

And there, behind that wall of skirts and sleeves, she paid the price.

The swineherd counted.

When he reached ten, he handed her the pot.

The Princess carried it away as if she had bought a jewel.

Another evening, the swineherd made something else: a tiny rattle that, when turned, could play every tune that had ever been sung in the city—marches, lullabies, dance songs, and the newest melodies from the streets.

The Princess heard it and came again.

“How much for the rattle?” she asked.

“One hundred kisses,” said the swineherd.

The Princess turned pale.

But the rattle’s music was bright and irresistible, and pride can be a hungry thing.

She ordered her ladies to hide her once more.

And she began to pay.

This time, however, the Emperor happened to walk through the yard.

He heard counting.

He saw the circle of ladies.

He pushed through and found his daughter trading kisses for a toy from a swineherd.

The Emperor’s face darkened.

“Out!” he shouted. “Out of my palace—both of you!”

The Princess began to cry.

The swineherd bowed.

And they were driven beyond the gates.

Outside, the evening air felt sharper. The road was dark. The Princess looked down at her soft cloak and polished shoes and realized how useless they were when there was nowhere to go.

“What will I do?” she whispered.

The swineherd stopped.

He looked at her—not cruelly, but sadly.

“Do you know who I am?” he asked.

Then he straightened and, with a simple motion, removed the rough cap that had hidden his face.

The Princess stared.

The swineherd was no swineherd.

He was the Prince.

“I came to you with a rose,” he said. “And a nightingale. You called them ‘only real.’ But you traded kisses for tricks and toys.”

The Princess’s cheeks burned.

She remembered the rose—how sweet it had smelled.

She remembered the nightingale’s song, and how, for a moment, it had made her feel something softer than pride.

“I… I didn’t understand,” she said.

The Prince shook his head.

“A kind heart values what is true,” he said. “I wanted a wife who could treasure simple beauty and honest gifts. I cannot marry someone who cannot see them.”

And the Prince walked away, leaving the Princess alone with the sound of the city behind her.

Some people say the Princess learned. Some say she did not. But the lesson remains:

That clever toys can shine for a moment.

And real roses and real songs—though plain to a proud eye—are sometimes the finest treasures of all.

The End.

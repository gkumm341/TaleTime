The Old House

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


On a quiet street where cobblestones had been smoothed by countless footsteps, there stood an old house.

Its roof sagged a little.

Its windows were small.

Its door creaked when the wind leaned on it.

New houses stood nearby—tall, straight, freshly painted. They looked proud and loud, as if they had something to prove.

The old house did not prove anything.

It simply stood.

And inside it lived an old man.

He did not talk much.

He did not visit.

And people on the street said, “He must be very lonely.”

In one of the new houses lived a little boy.

He liked to play outside.

He liked to notice things.

And when he passed the old house, he often wondered what it was like inside.

One afternoon, he stood near the old door and listened.

He heard a faint sound—someone moving carefully, as if every step mattered.

The boy took a breath and knocked.

At first, nothing happened.

Then the door opened a crack.

An old man peered out.

His hair was white.

His shoulders were thin.

But his eyes, though tired, were kind.

“Yes?” he asked.

The boy held out a small gift.

It was a drawing he had made.

It showed a bright sun.

It showed a little house.

It showed a smiling face.

It was not perfect.

But it was cheerful.

“I thought you might like it,” the boy said.

The old man blinked.

Then he opened the door wider.

“Come in,” he said softly.

Inside, the house smelled of old wood and warm tea.

There were chairs with worn arms.

There was a clock that ticked slowly.

And there were shelves full of things that had been kept for a long time.

The old man showed the boy objects that looked ordinary, yet seemed important in his hands.

A tin soldier.

A broken cup.

A small box of ribbons.

Each item had a story.

“This was from a birthday long ago,” he said.

“This belonged to a friend who used to laugh in this very room.”

The boy listened.

And he realized the old house was not empty at all.

It was full.

Full of memories.

When the boy stood to leave, the old man disappeared into another room and returned with something small.

It was a wooden bird, carved carefully, smooth from patient hands.

“For you,” the old man said.

The boy’s heart warmed.

He thanked him and ran home.

After that day, the boy passed the old house differently.

It did not look lonely.

It looked like a place that held stories.

He visited again.

And again.

And the old man smiled more.

Time went on.

One day, the street was quiet and the old house’s curtains did not move.

People said the old man had died.

Soon after, workmen came.

The old house was too old, they said.

It would be taken down.

The boy watched sadly as boards were removed and walls opened like pages.

He thought of the chair where the old man had sat.

He thought of the ticking clock.

He thought of the stories.

Then he remembered the wooden bird.

He held it carefully.

The house might be gone.

But something of it remained.

Because kindness can move from one place to another.

And because stories, once shared, do not disappear.

The End.

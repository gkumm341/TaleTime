The Shoes of Fortune

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


On a rainy evening in a great city, a party was held in a fine house. The rooms were warm, the candles bright, and the guests spoke of clever things.

Among the guests was an elderly lady who seemed ordinary at first glance. Yet she spoke as if she had walked through many worlds.

She had come from far away and, as it happened, she was connected to something few people ever noticed: the quiet work of fortune.

When she arrived, someone in the house happened to set a pair of shoes near the door—plain shoes, newly mended.

The elderly lady looked at them and said, almost absentmindedly, “Yes, those will do.”

She spoke a word under her breath, a word that sounded like a sigh.

No one heard.

No one saw.

But the shoes were changed.

From that moment, whoever wore them would find their wish answered.

Now, wishes sound wonderful when they are only thoughts.

But when a wish becomes real, it brings along the whole world—weather and consequence, comfort and cost.

The first person to slip into the shoes was a servant girl.

She had been running up and down stairs all evening, carrying trays and sweeping crumbs.

For a moment she stood in the hallway, listening to laughter from the parlor.

“If only,” she thought, “I could be a fine lady for just one day.”

The shoes tightened around her feet as if agreeing.

And in an instant, she found herself in a carriage rolling through the city.

People bowed.

Gentlemen tipped their hats.

Ladies looked at her gown and smiled politely, as if she had always belonged among them.

At first, the servant girl was dazzled.

But soon she realized she did not know the rules of this world.

She did not know the names.

She did not know what to say.

She did not know which fork to use or when to laugh.

And worst of all, she could not relax.

Every moment required pretending.

So when she finally had a quiet second, she whispered, “I wish I were back in my own kitchen.”

And she was.

She looked around at the simple room, at the warm kettle, at the familiar chair.

And she felt an unexpected relief.

Later, a young clerk found the shoes.

He was tired of the same streets, the same desk, the same narrow life.

“If only I could travel,” he thought. “If only I could be far away.”

The shoes answered.

The world tilted.

And suddenly he was far from home.

But the air was cold.

The language strange.

The streets unfamiliar.

And the loneliness came quickly, heavier than his suitcase.

He had wished for distance, but he had not wished for safety or belonging.

So he wished again.

“I wish I were home,” he said.

And home he went.

Another time, a man with a poet’s heart found the shoes.

He dreamed of a world where every thought could become visible, where dreams could be painted in the air.

He wished for it.

And for a moment the world became a crowded sky of images and voices. Ideas swirled around him like a storm of bright ribbons.

At first he was astonished.

Then he was overwhelmed.

Because not all thoughts are kind.

Not all dreams are gentle.

And even beauty becomes noise when there is no silence to rest inside.

“I wish to be back,” he cried.

And he returned, trembling a little, grateful for the quiet page.

News of the shoes spread.

More people tried them.

Some wished to be admired.

Some wished to be powerful.

Some wished to be young again.

And each time, the wish arrived carrying its shadow.

At last, the shoes were noticed by someone who did not hunger for grand things.

This person held them and thought carefully.

“If these shoes must grant wishes,” they said, “then let the wish be a wise one.”

So they wished for what is hardest to wish for, because it is so simple:

To be content.

To be able to do today’s work without bitterness.

To sleep peacefully.

To love and be loved.

And as they stood there, nothing magical happened.

No carriage appeared.

No strange country opened beneath their feet.

No dazzling world rose in front of their eyes.

Because the wish did not need to fly away.

It could be lived.

The shoes, which had caused so much confusion, were set aside.

Not as a toy.

Not as a treasure.

But as a reminder that wishing is easy…

and wisdom is rarer.

The End.

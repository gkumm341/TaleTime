The Leap-Frog

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


In a green ditch near a garden path, where reeds whispered and lily leaves floated like small boats, a leap-frog lived.

He was quick.

He was clever.

And, most of all, he thought very highly of himself.

When he jumped, he jumped as if the whole ditch should applaud.

When he croaked at night, he croaked as if he were giving a concert.

And when he looked at his own reflection in the water, he felt sure he was meant for great things.

One day, a lady-frog arrived.

She was calm and graceful, with bright eyes that looked as if they noticed more than reeds and flies.

The leap-frog puffed himself up.

“At last,” he thought, “someone worthy of me!”

He hopped closer and bowed, as much as a frog can bow.

“I am the finest frog in the ditch,” he announced. “You should marry me.”

The lady-frog did not laugh, and she did not flatter.

She simply said, “We shall see.”

Soon after, two visitors arrived: the lady-frog’s aunts.

They were old snails, slow and careful.

They wore moss on their shells like little shawls, and they blinked as if each blink were a decision.

“So,” said one snail, “you wish to marry our niece.”

“Of course,” said the leap-frog.

“Then tell us,” said the other snail, “what sort of husband you will be.”

The leap-frog launched into a speech.

He spoke of his leaps.

He spoke of his strong legs.

He spoke of his loud songs.

He spoke of how everyone noticed him.

The snails listened politely.

Then one asked gently, “Will you be steady? Will you be kind? Will you keep your temper when the water is low and the day is hot?”

The leap-frog did not like those questions.

They sounded slow.

They sounded ordinary.

He wanted admiration, not examination.

To impress them, he decided to perform.

He leapt.

And leapt again.

He jumped so high that a dragonfly darted away in surprise.

He landed with a splash and looked around, expecting applause.

The snails nodded.

“Very lively,” one said.

But the lady-frog looked thoughtful rather than amazed.

Days passed.

The leap-frog waited for his proposal to be accepted.

He imagined the wedding.

He imagined being admired.

But the lady-frog did not choose him.

She listened to the garden.

She spoke kindly to those who were kind.

And when she finally hopped away to begin her life, she went quietly, without boasting or drama.

The leap-frog felt a sting.

He tried to convince himself she had been wrong.

But as the nights went on, he remembered the snails’ questions.

He began to wonder if being loud was not the same as being good.

He did not change in a day.

Pride is a stubborn thing.

But little by little he listened more.

He boasted less.

And sometimes, when he saw another creature trying hard to impress, he understood something he had not understood before.

That a calm heart can be more impressive than a high jump.

And that the best kind of growing is quiet, slow… and real.

The End.

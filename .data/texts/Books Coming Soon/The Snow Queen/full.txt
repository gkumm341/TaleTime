The Snow Queen

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


Long before Gerda and Kay were born, there was a strange story told in whispers.

It was said that a wicked creature had once made a mirror.

This mirror did not show people as they truly were.

Instead, it made everything good look small and foolish.

It made everything ugly look large and important.

Kindness looked weak.

Love looked silly.

Beauty looked ordinary.

And when anyone looked into it, their heart grew colder.

One day the mirror broke.

It shattered into countless pieces.

The splinters flew across the world like dust.

Some pieces fell into people’s eyes.

Some pieces slid into people’s hearts.

And wherever a splinter landed, it did the same thing the mirror had done.

In a little town, two children lived in neighboring houses.

Their names were Gerda and Kay.

Between their windows grew roses. In summer, the roses climbed happily. In winter, they were protected from the frost.

Gerda and Kay were the kind of friends who could turn an ordinary day into an adventure.

They played among the flowers.

They sat by the warm stove.

They listened to stories.

One night, someone told them about the Snow Queen.

“She is cold as winter,” the story said. “Her palace is made of ice. And her kiss can numb a heart.”

Kay laughed.

Gerda held her hands closer to the warmth.

Not long after, Kay rubbed his eye.

He winced.

Something tiny and sharp had slipped in.

It was a splinter from the broken mirror.

And while he blinked and complained, another splinter—smaller still—found its way into his heart.

From that day, Kay changed.

The roses looked “stupid” to him.

Gerda’s stories sounded “childish.”

He laughed at kind people.

He admired cold cleverness.

Gerda did not understand what had happened. She only knew her friend was still there, and yet not there at all.

Then winter came.

Snow fell like feathers.

The river froze.

The air glittered.

One evening, Kay went out with his sled.

In the street, snow swirled so thickly that the lamplight looked far away.

Suddenly, a carriage appeared in the snow—white and shining.

In it sat a tall woman wrapped in pale fur.

Her eyes were bright and cold.

Her face was beautiful in a way that felt dangerous.

She smiled at Kay.

And before he could move, she leaned down and kissed him.

At once, Kay stopped shivering.

He felt strong.

He felt fearless.

He felt as if the winter belonged to him.

Then she kissed him again.

And the second kiss was colder than the first.

After it, Kay forgot to be afraid.

Forgot to miss home.

Forgot, for a while, even Gerda.

The Snow Queen took him into her carriage, and the wind carried them away.

When Gerda heard Kay was gone, people said he must have fallen into the river.

But Gerda did not believe it.

She went to the river and asked it.

“Have you taken Kay?” she whispered.

The river only rippled.

Gerda threw her red shoes into the water as an offering, begging the river to return her friend.

The river did not.

But it did send her onward.

Gerda’s journey began.

She wandered beyond the town.

She met a kind old woman in a garden who tried to keep her there, surrounding her with flowers so she would forget her search.

But when Gerda saw roses that reminded her of home, her heart woke up again.

She ran.

She asked birds.

She asked trees.

She asked the wind.

Sometimes she was helped.

Sometimes she was misled.

She met a talking crow who told her of a boy in a palace.

Her hope flared.

She went to the palace and found… not Kay, but another kind boy, and a Princess and Prince who listened to her story.

They gave her warm clothes.

They gave her a carriage.

They gave her food for the road.

And they wished her courage.

But the world was not only kind.

On the road, robbers attacked the carriage.

Gerda was taken into a robber’s den.

There she met a robber girl with wild hair and a fierce temper.

The robber girl might have been cruel, but something in Gerda’s tears and steady love touched her.

She listened.

She looked at Gerda.

And at last she said, “I will not keep you.”

She gave Gerda a reindeer and sent her north.

“The Snow Queen lives far away,” the reindeer told Gerda as it carried her through icy wind. “But I will take you as far as I can.”

They reached the coldest lands, where the air was sharp as glass.

Gerda met wise women who wrote messages on dried fish and bark, passing guidance along the road.

At last, the reindeer brought her to the Snow Queen’s palace.

It stood shining in the snow—beautiful, vast, and silent.

The walls were ice.

The floors were ice.

The wind itself seemed to live there.

Gerda stepped inside.

Her breath froze in the air.

And there, in a great hall, she saw Kay.

He sat alone, pale and still, trying to arrange sharp pieces of ice into a word.

He moved the ice again and again, as if he must solve a puzzle to earn his freedom.

His eyes were cold.

He did not look up.

“Kay!” Gerda cried.

At first, he did not recognize her.

The splinter in his heart held him.

The splinter in his eye showed him the world through cold glass.

Gerda ran to him and threw her arms around him.

“Don’t you remember me?” she whispered.

Kay’s face remained distant.

Then Gerda began to cry.

Warm tears fell onto Kay’s chest.

And the warmth—simple, human warmth—did what the Snow Queen’s kisses could not.

It melted the icy splinter in his heart.

Kay gasped.

His eyes filled.

The splinter in his eye loosened.

And suddenly he saw Gerda clearly.

He remembered roses.

He remembered summer.

He remembered home.

He began to cry, and the last shard slid away.

Kay and Gerda held each other, shaking with relief.

The palace did not seem so powerful now.

Because love—steady, stubborn, and real—had broken its spell.

Together they walked out.

The wind howled, but it could not stop them.

They traveled south.

They found their way back to the town, to the roses at the windows.

And when they sat together again, they were older.

Not only because time had passed.

But because they understood something.

That coldness can enter a heart.

That cruelty can look clever.

But that warmth and faithfulness can lead someone home.

The End.

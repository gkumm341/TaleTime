The Emperor’s New Clothes

HANS CHRISTIAN ANDERSEN


Long ago, in a bright and bustling city, there lived an Emperor who cared more for fine clothes than for anything else.

He did not care much for the theater, or for parades, or for his soldiers. If the sun shone, he wanted a new outfit for the morning. If the clouds came, he wanted another outfit for the afternoon. If visitors arrived, he wanted to appear in something even finer.

“The Emperor is in his dressing room,” the people would say, because he was there so often.

One day, two strangers came to the city. They looked confident and spoke sweetly.

“We are weavers,” they announced, “and we can make cloth so delicate, so splendid, so rare, that it has a most extraordinary quality.”

The Emperor leaned forward.

“What quality?” he asked.

“Our cloth,” they said, “cannot be seen by anyone who is foolish… or by anyone who is unfit for their position.”

The Emperor’s eyes widened.

“That would be perfect,” he thought. “If I wore such clothing, I could discover who in my city is clever and who is not. I could see who deserves their job.”

And, of course, he also imagined how magnificent he would look.

So he gave the strangers a grand room in the palace, he provided them with the finest silk and gold thread, and he ordered them to begin at once.

The two weavers set up looms.

They sat at the empty frames and moved their hands quickly.

They snipped with scissors.

They measured with tape.

They nodded at one another.

And they asked for more and more silk, and more and more gold thread.

But all the silk and gold went into their own pockets.

After a few days, the Emperor grew impatient.

“I will send someone to see how the work is going,” he said. “But… I will not go myself yet.”

For although he trusted he was wise and fit to rule, the thought of not seeing the cloth made his stomach tighten.

He called his oldest minister.

“Go to the weavers,” the Emperor said, “and report to me on their progress.”

The minister walked proudly into the room.

He looked.

He blinked.

He stared.

There was nothing on the looms. Nothing at all.

His heart sank.

“I cannot see anything,” he thought. “Does that mean I am foolish? Unfit? Impossible!”

So he did what many people do when they are afraid of the truth: he pretended.

“Oh!” he said loudly. “It is… it is splendid!”

The weavers smiled.

“Splendid, isn’t it?” they said. “Look at the pattern. Look at the colors.”

The minister leaned forward as if inspecting every thread.

“Marvelous,” he said. “Absolutely marvelous.”

Then he hurried back to the Emperor.

“Your Majesty,” he declared, “the cloth is the most beautiful I have ever seen.”

The Emperor felt pleased… and also uneasy.

“I will send someone else,” he decided.

Soon another official went, and then another.

Each one saw nothing.

Each one praised what they could not see.

Each one returned to the Emperor with glowing words.

At last, the Emperor could wait no longer.

He marched to the room himself, surrounded by attendants.

The doors opened.

The looms stood there, empty as air.

The Emperor froze.

“I see nothing,” he thought. “Am I foolish? Am I unfit?”

But he lifted his chin.

“Oh! It is magnificent,” he said.

All the attendants immediately agreed.

“Magnificent, magnificent,” they echoed.

The weavers began to “finish” the outfit.

They held up invisible trousers.

They adjusted an invisible coat.

They placed an invisible cloak on the Emperor’s shoulders.

“Light as a spiderweb,” they said.

The Emperor stood before the mirror.

He looked at his own reflection and tried to believe the lie with all his might.

Soon it was time for the great parade.

The Emperor walked under flags and banners.

His attendants pretended to hold up the invisible train of his robe.

The crowd cheered and clapped.

Not because they saw splendid cloth, but because they feared being thought foolish.

Then, in the middle of the cheering, a small voice rose up.

“But he isn’t wearing anything!”

It was a child.

The crowd murmured.

“A child says he has nothing on,” someone whispered.

“He isn’t wearing anything,” another repeated.

The words moved through the crowd like wind through tall grass.

The Emperor felt a shock, cold as water.

He knew it was true.

But he had come so far.

He was already in the street.

And the whole city was watching.

So he held himself even straighter and walked on.

His attendants followed, still pretending to hold the train.

And the people, now seeing the truth clearly, learned what the child had shown them:

That honesty can be simple.

And that fear of looking foolish can make even a whole city pretend.
